The adab of the student of knowledge

What the classical manuals of learning say about intention, conduct with teachers, and the habits that make knowledge settle and bear fruit.

Himaayah Schools · 24 October 2026 · 3 min read

Muslim scholars wrote a distinct genre of books on adab al-ʿilm, the conduct of learning. Works such as al-Nawawī's al-Tibyān, Ibn Jamāʿah's Tadhkirat al-Sāmiʿ wa-l-Mutakallim and al-Zarnūjī's Taʿlīm al-Mutaʿallim treat the way a student learns as inseparable from what is learned. Their advice is practical and surprisingly modern: it concerns motivation, attention, relationships and routine. This article summarises the main principles.

Intention comes first

Every manual begins with niyyah. The student is asked to seek knowledge for the sake of Allah, to act upon it and to benefit others, rather than for status or argument. Ibn Jamāʿah notes that a sincere intention also makes study lighter, because the student is not working for an audience. In practical terms, renewing the intention before each lesson turns study from a chore into worship.

Conduct with the teacher

The classical texts place great weight on the relationship between student and teacher. The student greets the teacher, listens without interrupting, asks questions at a suitable moment and in a respectful way, and accepts correction without resentment. None of this is mere etiquette. A student who is comfortable being corrected receives more correction, and correction is how errors in recitation, reasoning and behaviour are removed.

  • Arrive on time and prepared, with the previous lesson revised.
  • Ask genuine questions; do not ask to test or embarrass the teacher.
  • Write down corrections and review them before the next lesson.
  • Make duʿāʾ for your teachers.

Gradual learning and consistency

Al-Zarnūjī advises the student to take a portion he can master, to repeat it until it is firm, and only then to increase. He warns against starting many books and finishing none. This matches what cognitive research says about spaced, cumulative practice: small amounts learned well and revisited regularly outlast large amounts covered quickly. The famous duʿāʾ taught in the Qur'an, rabbi zidnī ʿilmā, asks for increase, and increase presupposes a foundation.

Guarding the heart and the time

The manuals connect learning with character. Al-Nawawī warns against envy of other students and against pride in what one has memorised. Ibn Jamāʿah advises the student to protect his time, to choose companions who are serious about learning and to avoid excessive food and sleep that dull the mind. The modern equivalents are obvious: a phone in another room, a fixed study time and friends who encourage effort.

Questions, notes and review

The manuals also give detailed advice on how to study. Al-Zarnūjī recommends writing down what one learns and reviewing it the same day, the next day and again later, a pattern that closely resembles what modern researchers call spaced review. Ibn Jamāʿah advises the student to ask about what he does not understand rather than pretend to understand, and to choose a teacher known for both knowledge and good character.

For a student of the Qur'an today, these principles translate into a simple routine: write the corrections from each lesson, recite the new portion several times on the day it is learned, revise it before the next lesson, and bring questions instead of silence. Small, faithful habits of this kind are what the scholars meant by adab in learning.

Acting on what is learned

Finally, the scholars insist that knowledge is measured by its effect. A student who memorises verses about honesty and then cheats in an examination has missed the point. Teachers in the classical tradition expected students to show their learning in prayer, manners and service. For parents and students today, this is a useful test of any programme: is the student becoming better, not only more informed?
