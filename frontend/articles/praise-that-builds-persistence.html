Praise that builds persistence

Why praising effort and strategy helps children keep going after mistakes, what the research found, and phrases parents can use.

Himaayah Schools · 24 October 2026 · 5 min read

صَبْرPatience

Parents praise children because they want them to feel capable. But the kind of praise matters. A well-known line of research suggests that praising children for being clever can make them more fragile when work becomes difficult, while praising effort and strategy encourages persistence. This article explains the findings, their limits and how to apply them at home.

What the research found

In a series of experiments, Mueller and Dweck gave children a set of problems and then praised them either for their intelligence ("You must be smart at this") or for their effort ("You must have worked hard"). After a harder set on which everyone struggled, children praised for intelligence enjoyed the task less, persisted less and performed worse on a later set. Children praised for effort were more likely to choose challenging tasks and to attribute difficulty to insufficient effort rather than lack of ability.

A balanced reading of the evidence

Later studies of 'growth mindset' interventions have found smaller effects than early reports suggested, and effects vary between students. Praise is not magic. But the core practical point has held up well: feedback that tells a child what they did that worked, and what to try next, is more useful than a label. Effort alone is not the goal either; praising effort on a strategy that is not working can mislead.

Phrases that help

  • "You kept going on that verse until it was right."
  • "Breaking the question into steps really worked."
  • "That was hard, and you did not give up. What will you try next time?"
  • "Your revision every day is why this sūrah is so firm."

Responding to mistakes

How parents react to mistakes teaches as much as how they praise success. A calm response that treats the mistake as information, "Let's see where it went wrong", makes children more willing to attempt difficult work. Anger or mockery teaches children to hide mistakes or avoid challenge. Islamic teaching on gentleness with children fits this well: firmness about standards, gentleness in how they are taught.

Praise in the Qur'an class

In memorisation, where progress can feel slow, specific praise matters a great deal. A teacher or parent who notices that a child's makhārij have improved, that a difficult verse is now fluent, or that revision has become consistent, helps the child see progress they might otherwise miss. General praise such as "You are so clever" gives no information about what to keep doing.

Feedback research by Hattie and Timperley distinguishes feedback about the task, the process and self-regulation from praise of the person. The first three help learning; the last does little on its own. The practical rule is easy to remember: describe what the child did and what it achieved.

Praise in the Qur'an class

In a memorisation class, praise can focus on what a student actually did: coming prepared, correcting a mistake after being told once, revising a portion that was weak, or reciting with careful tajwīd. These are things a child can repeat tomorrow. Saying 'you are a natural hafidh' offers the child nothing to act on and may lead to fear of failing when the next portion is hard.

Setting goals together

Children persist more when goals are clear, close and their own. Sit with your child and choose one small goal for the week, such as revising a particular sūrah every day. At the end of the week, look at what happened together, praise the effort, and choose the next goal. This teaches the child to plan and reflect, which are skills for life.

Praise and rewards

Small rewards can be helpful at the start, but rewards for everything can turn effort into something done only for the prize. Use rewards occasionally, for real milestones, and let warm attention and encouragement do most of the work. A child who learns to be proud of effort does not need a reward each time.

Teaching children to praise themselves

The long-term goal is a child who can recognise their own effort without waiting for an adult. Ask questions such as: what are you proud of in this work, what was hard, and what would you do differently? A child who can answer these builds an inner sense of progress that lasts long after the teacher or parent is no longer there to give feedback.

Gratitude and praise in the family

In a home where adults thank one another, notice kindness and say 'jazakAllahu khayran' sincerely, children learn to see and name good effort. Praise then becomes part of the family culture, not a tool for managing behaviour.

Praise the person sparingly, the work often

Children still need to know that they are loved regardless of results. Warm affection should be unconditional; praise for work should be specific and honest. Over time, children who hear what effective work looks like learn to recognise it themselves, which is the real aim: a learner who can evaluate their own progress and keep going.

Handling failure and setbacks

When a child fails a test or struggles with a portion, the first response matters. Acknowledge the disappointment, then move to what can be learned: what went well, what to change, and what to try next. Children who learn that setbacks are information, not verdicts, are more willing to try again.

Praise for siblings and groups

With more than one child, praise can create rivalry if it is always comparative. Praise each child for their own progress, and avoid statements like 'you are better than your brother'. In a group class, a teacher can praise the group's effort and each member's growth, so that the class becomes a team rather than a ranking.

Praise for older children and teenagers

Older children can be embarrassed by public praise and can see through empty compliments. Be specific and private, and treat them as partners: 'I noticed how you organised your revision timetable; that took thought.' Respect their growing independence by asking what they plan next rather than telling them what to do.

Keeping a record of progress

A simple record of milestones, such as sūrahs learned or test scores improved, helps children see that effort pays off. Look at it together from time to time, especially when the child feels stuck, to remind them how far they have come.

Frequently asked questions

Is it wrong to tell a child they are clever?

An occasional compliment is fine; the concern is making it the main message. Link praise to what the child did.

What if the child does not respond to praise?

Some children are uncomfortable being praised in front of others, so try private encouragement.

Can too much praise be harmful?

Yes, if it is vague or inflated, because children can tell when it is not genuine.

Key points

  • Praise effort, strategy and improvement, with specific words.
  • Treat mistakes as part of learning.
  • Be sincere and use rewards sparingly.
