Reading comprehension for Use of English

Why vocabulary and wide reading matter most, a step-by-step approach to comprehension passages, and how to practise.

Himaayah Schools · 24 October 2026 · 7 min read

قِرَاءَةReading

Use of English is compulsory in the UTME, and comprehension passages are a large part of it. Many candidates find them time-consuming and unpredictable. Comprehension depends on two things: the language knowledge a reader brings, especially vocabulary, and the strategies used while reading. Both can be developed. This article explains what research says about reading and offers a practical approach.

Vocabulary is the foundation

Readers need to know the great majority of words in a text to understand it comfortably. Research by Nation estimated that reading a range of authentic English texts requires a vocabulary of several thousand word families. The practical implication is that comprehension improves most through sustained reading over months, which builds vocabulary in context, rather than through last-minute tricks.

A step-by-step approach to a passage

  • Skim the questions first to know what to look for.
  • Read the passage once for the main idea and the writer's purpose.
  • For each question, find the exact sentence that supports your answer.
  • Eliminate options that are true in general but not stated in the passage.
  • For vocabulary questions, substitute each option into the sentence and test the meaning.

Active reading habits

Long-standing study methods such as Robinson's SQ3R (survey, question, read, recite, review) share a principle: understanding improves when the reader actively asks and answers questions instead of passively running eyes over the text. For daily practice, students can read a newspaper editorial or an article, summarise it in two sentences, note unfamiliar words with their meaning in context, and explain the writer's attitude.

Managing time

Comprehension passages can consume too much time if a student reads every word slowly several times. Practising with a timer, and learning to locate answers quickly by scanning for key words and their synonyms, keeps the rest of the paper safe. Students should also practise the other sections of Use of English, such as lexis and structure and oral forms, which reward regular drilling.

Building vocabulary deliberately

Alongside wide reading, a small amount of deliberate vocabulary study helps. When a student meets an unfamiliar word, they can note it with the sentence in which it appeared, guess its meaning from context, check a dictionary and review the list regularly. Learning common prefixes, suffixes and Latin and Greek roots, such as bene-, mal-, -logy and -cide, helps students work out new words in the examination.

Synonyms and antonyms appear frequently in Use of English. Grouping words by meaning, for example words for anger, kindness or speed, makes them easier to remember than an alphabetical list.

Types of questions and how to approach them

Comprehension questions generally fall into three kinds. Some ask for information stated directly in the passage, and the answer can be found by scanning. Others ask what is implied, which requires reading between the lines and using clues in the text. A third kind asks about the writer's purpose or tone, which needs a sense of the whole passage. Recognising the kind of question tells you where to look.

Practising with real passages

Practise with one passage a day. Read it once for the main idea, then answer the questions, then check the answers and find in the text the sentence that supports each one. For wrong answers, ask what led you astray: a word you did not know, a careless reading, or an assumption that the text did not support. This habit builds accuracy and speed together.

Building vocabulary from what you read

Keep a small notebook of new words from your reading. Write the word, a short meaning, and the sentence where you found it. Review the list every few days. Words learned in context are easier to remember than words learned from a list alone, and they appear again in later passages.

Inference: reading between the lines

Many marks are lost on questions that ask what a passage implies. To answer them, ask what the writer assumes, what attitude a word choice shows, and what must be true for a statement to make sense. The correct answer is always supported by something in the text; if an option goes beyond what the passage supports, it is probably a trap. Practising this with short extracts, and justifying each answer in a sentence, sharpens the skill quickly.

Vocabulary in context

Passage questions often ask the meaning of a word as used in the text. A word can have several meanings, so the surrounding sentence decides. Try replacing the word with each option and see which one keeps the sense. Notice also prefixes and suffixes, which give clues: 'un-' often reverses a meaning, '-less' means without. This approach lets you work out many unfamiliar words during the exam.

Reading as a lifelong skill

Strong reading comprehension serves far beyond the UTME: in every university course, in professional life and in understanding religious texts and translations. A student who builds a daily reading habit during exam preparation gains a skill that keeps paying back for decades.

Summary and main idea questions

Some questions ask for the main idea or the best summary. The main idea is what the whole passage is about, not a single detail. Look at the first and last paragraphs, notice what the writer keeps returning to, and be wary of options that are true but cover only part of the passage. A good summary is shorter than the original but keeps its central point.

Tone, purpose and attitude

Writers have attitudes: they may be critical, hopeful, ironic or neutral. Word choice reveals them. Words such as 'regrettably' or 'fortunately' show a view, while a flat description of facts is neutral. Ask what the writer wants the reader to think or do, and let the evidence in the text decide, not your own opinion on the topic.

Reading widely to prepare

Alongside exam practice, read widely: newspaper articles, short stories, essays and explanatory pieces. A student who reads for twenty minutes a day builds the vocabulary, speed and feel for style that no cramming can replace. Choose material you find interesting, since enjoyment keeps you reading.

Frequently asked questions

How can I read faster without losing understanding?

Read the questions first, skim for the main idea, and then read for detail where needed.

What if the passage has many unknown words?

Use the surrounding sentences to guess the meaning, and move on; you do not need every word to answer correctly.

Should I read English every day?

Yes, even short passages, because regular reading is the surest way to grow vocabulary and speed.

Reading the questions before the passage

Skim the questions first so that you know what to look for as you read. This does not replace reading the passage carefully, but it focuses your attention on details that will be tested. Mark the key word in each question, such as a name, a date or a contrast word, and look for it in the text. When you find the relevant lines, read the sentences before and after them as well, because the answer often depends on context.

Eliminating wrong options

In multiple-choice questions, wrong options are often almost right. Typical traps include an option that is true but not stated in the passage, one that uses a word from the text but changes the meaning, and one that goes further than the writer does. Cross out the options you can show to be wrong, and choose from what remains. If two seem possible, return to the passage and find the line that supports one but not the other.

Keeping a vocabulary notebook

Write each new word with a short meaning and the sentence where you met it. Revise the notebook for five minutes a day, and cover the meanings to test yourself. A few words a day, learned in context, add up to hundreds within a few months, and they appear again in the passages you read.

Practising under time pressure

Once you are comfortable with the method, begin timing yourself. Allow a set number of minutes for each passage and its questions, and note which type of question takes you longest. Speed comes after accuracy, so do not rush at the beginning. A steady routine of one timed passage a day builds both fluency and confidence.

Reading for pleasure

The best preparation is a habit of reading English that you enjoy: a news article, a short story, a biography. The more varied your reading, the more at home you will feel with unfamiliar passages in the exam. Choose material slightly above your comfort level and read a little every day.

Learning from mistakes in practice passages

After each practice passage, review every wrong answer and write down why you chose it. Was it a vocabulary gap, a rushed reading, or a trap you fell for? Patterns will appear, and each pattern tells you what to practise. Reviewing carefully for ten minutes often teaches more than starting another passage immediately.

Staying calm with long passages

A long passage looks harder than it is. Break it into paragraphs, summarise each in a few words in the margin, and answer the questions with those notes beside you. Calm, methodical reading is more effective than anxious speed.

Key points

  • Read the passage for the main idea, then answer with evidence from the text.
  • Build vocabulary from reading, in context.
  • Manage your time, and practise regularly with real passages.
