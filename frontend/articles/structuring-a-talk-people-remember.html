Structuring a talk people remember

Classical rhetoric and modern practice on finding a central message, organising a talk, writing for the ear and managing nerves.

Himaayah Schools · 24 October 2026 · 3 min read

Most people can speak; far fewer can be understood and remembered by an audience. The difference is rarely talent. It is structure, preparation and practice, all of which can be learned. This article draws on classical rhetoric and modern communication practice to set out how to build a clear, persuasive talk.

Start with one sentence

Before writing a talk, state its central message in a single sentence. If the message cannot be expressed in one sentence, the audience will struggle to identify it. Every part of the talk should support that sentence; material that does not support it should be removed, however interesting.

Ethos, pathos and logos

Aristotle's Rhetoric identifies three means of persuasion: the character and credibility of the speaker (ethos), the emotions of the audience (pathos) and the argument itself (logos). Effective talks use all three. Credibility comes from preparation, honesty and relevant experience; emotion from concrete stories and examples; argument from clear reasoning and evidence.

A reliable structure

One widely taught structure for persuasive speaking is Monroe's Motivated Sequence, developed by Alan Monroe in the 1930s:

  • Attention: open with a question, a fact or a short story that makes the audience want to listen.
  • Need: show the problem and why it matters to this audience.
  • Satisfaction: present your solution or main idea.
  • Visualisation: help the audience picture the result of accepting it, or of ignoring it.
  • Action: tell them clearly what to do next.

For informative talks a simpler pattern works: tell the audience what you will cover, cover it in two or three clearly signposted points, and summarise.

Write for the ear

A talk is heard once, at the speaker's pace. Listeners cannot reread a sentence they missed. Use short sentences, concrete words and signposts ("there are three reasons", "the second reason is"), and repeat the central message at the beginning and the end.

Openings and closings

Audiences remember beginnings and endings best. The first thirty seconds decide whether people listen; the last thirty decide what they take away. Avoid opening with apologies or housekeeping. Open instead with a question, a striking fact or a short story that leads to the central message. Close by restating the message and, where appropriate, asking the audience to do one specific thing.

Evidence and stories

Arguments persuade the mind and stories engage attention; strong talks combine both. Support each main point with one piece of evidence, such as a figure, a quotation or an example, and one concrete story or illustration. Name your sources: citing them accurately is both honest and persuasive.

Clarity in the Islamic tradition

Clarity of speech has long been valued in Islamic scholarship, from the sciences of balāghah to the etiquette of the khuṭbah. When Mūsā عليه السلام was sent to Pharaoh, he asked:

The aim of good speaking is not display but understanding.

Practise aloud, then on camera

Rehearsing silently is not enough. Practise aloud at full volume, time yourself, and record a run-through. A recording reveals habits that are invisible from the inside: filler words, rushing, looking down, distracting movements. Practise in front of a small audience before the real one.

Delivery: voice, pace and presence

  • Speak more slowly than feels natural; nervous speakers almost always rush.
  • Use pauses deliberately, especially before and after your most important sentence.
  • Vary your tone to mark questions, emphasis and transitions.
  • Look at individuals in different parts of the audience, holding each gaze for a sentence.
  • Stand still when making a key point; move only with purpose.

Managing nerves

Nervousness is normal and does not disappear with experience; it becomes manageable. Preparation is the most effective remedy. Slow breathing before speaking, a strong prepared opening and attention to the audience's needs rather than to oneself all help.

Handling questions

Questions are an opportunity to clarify the message. Listen to the whole question, repeat or rephrase it so everyone hears it, and answer briefly. If you do not know the answer, say so and offer to follow up. Prepare in advance for the two or three questions you are most likely to be asked.
