Raising teenagers with deen and direction

What developmental research says about adolescence, why peers and mentors matter so much, and how faith becomes conviction rather than habit.

Himaayah Schools · 24 October 2026 · 3 min read

Adolescence is the period in which a young person begins to form an identity of their own. The values, habits and relationships built between roughly thirteen and nineteen often shape adulthood. For Muslim families, the task is to help teenagers hold their faith as their own conviction rather than as a set of household rules, while developing the competence and confidence to contribute.

What changes in adolescence

Developmental research describes adolescence as a time of rapid change in the brain's reward and social systems, while the regions involved in planning and impulse control continue to mature into the early twenties. In practice, teenagers are highly sensitive to peers and to approval, and more willing to take risks, especially in groups (Steinberg, 2008). The psychologist Erik Erikson described the central task of this stage as forming a coherent identity, as opposed to confusion about one's role and values.

These findings do not mean teenagers cannot be responsible. They mean that environment, relationships and structure matter a great deal: teenagers tend to rise to clear expectations set by adults they trust, and to the standards of the group they belong to.

Faith as conviction, not only habit

Practices learned in childhood, such as prayer and recitation, need to be understood as well as performed. Teenagers ask why, and the question deserves a serious answer. Studying ʿaqīdah, sīrah and the wisdom behind rulings at a level suited to their age helps them hold their faith with understanding, and equips them to answer the questions they meet from peers and online.

The peer group matters

Because teenagers are so responsive to peers, one of the most effective things a parent can do is help them belong to good company. The Prophet ﷺ compared a good companion to a carrier of musk and a bad one to a blacksmith working the bellows (Ṣaḥīḥ al-Bukhārī 5534; Ṣaḥīḥ Muslim 2628). A structured community of motivated young Muslims gives teenagers a group whose norms support their growth.

Competence builds confidence

Digital life

Much of a teenager's social life now happens on a phone. Rather than relying only on bans, which teenagers often work around, families do better with agreed principles: devices out of bedrooms at night, phones away during prayer, study and meals, and open conversation about what they see online. Parents who model the same habits are more persuasive than those who do not.

Responding to hard questions

Teenagers encounter challenges to faith from peers, school and the internet. A question is not a rejection, and treating it as one closes the conversation. It is better to welcome the question, admit when an answer requires study, and look for it together with a knowledgeable teacher. The Islamic scholarly tradition has engaged seriously with difficult questions for centuries, and showing teenagers that tradition builds confidence.

Mentors beyond the home

Teenagers often listen to a trusted adult outside the family more readily than to their parents, even when the message is the same. Mentors, such as teachers, facilitators and older students, can reinforce what parents teach, notice difficulties early and model a balanced life of faith and ambition.

Practical steps for parents

  • Keep conversation open: ask for their views and listen before correcting.
  • Agree clear, reasonable expectations about prayer, study and screen use, and explain the reasons.
  • Give real responsibility at home and in the community.
  • Help them find a structured group of peers and mentors.
  • Acknowledge effort and growth, not only results.

When to seek more support

Most difficulties in adolescence are part of normal growth. Some changes, however, call for closer attention: withdrawal from family and friends, a sharp fall in school performance, persistent low mood or changes in sleep and eating. In these cases, talk with the teenager calmly, involve trusted teachers, and seek help from a qualified professional where needed. Seeking help early is a form of care, not a failure.
