Sleep and learning in teenagers

How much sleep teenagers need, why their body clock shifts later, how sleep consolidates memory, and what families can do.

Himaayah Schools · 24 October 2026 · 2 min read

Teenagers preparing for examinations often cut sleep to make time for study. The evidence suggests this is a poor trade. Sleep is when the brain consolidates what was learned during the day, and chronic sleep loss impairs attention, mood and memory. This article explains how much sleep adolescents need, why they struggle to get it, and practical steps for families.

How much sleep is enough

The American Academy of Sleep Medicine recommends 8 to 10 hours of sleep in each 24 hours for teenagers aged 13 to 18, and 9 to 12 hours for children aged 6 to 12. Many students sleep far less on school nights and try to catch up at weekends, which shifts their body clock further and makes Monday mornings harder.

Why teenagers fall asleep later

During adolescence the body's internal clock shifts later, so teenagers naturally feel sleepy later in the evening than younger children. Carskadon describes a 'perfect storm': this biological delay combines with early school start times, homework, social pressure and evening screen use. The result is a teenager who is not lazy but genuinely short of sleep.

Sleep consolidates memory

Research reviewed by Stickgold and others shows that sleep plays an active role in memory consolidation: material studied before sleep is better retained after a full night than after an equal period awake. For a student, this means the last review of the day is most useful when followed by proper sleep, and that an all-night study session before an exam undermines the very memory the student is relying on.

What families can do

  • Agree a consistent bedtime and wake time, including weekends where possible.
  • Charge phones outside the bedroom and stop screens an hour before sleep.
  • Plan study earlier in the evening, and keep the last half hour for light review.
  • Use the Sunnah rhythm: sleeping after ʿIshāʾ and rising for Fajr supports an earlier schedule.
  • Avoid caffeine and heavy meals late in the evening.
A study plan that leaves room for sleep

Academy students follow a weekly timetable and regular mocks, so revision does not pile up at the end.

Signs of sleep deprivation

Parents can watch for signs that a teenager is not getting enough sleep: difficulty waking on school days, falling asleep in class or during online lessons, irritability, frequent headaches, and sleeping much longer at weekends. Poor sleep can also look like poor motivation. Before concluding that a student is lazy, it is worth asking how much sleep they are actually getting.

Short daytime naps of about twenty minutes can help a tired student, but long naps late in the afternoon make it harder to fall asleep at night. The Sunnah of the qaylūlah, a short rest around midday, fits well with what sleep researchers recommend.

Before an examination

The night before an examination should be for light review and early sleep, not new material. Students who have studied steadily for weeks lose little by stopping early; students who have not studied gain little from an exhausted night. Parents can help most by making the evening calm and the morning unhurried.

Sources and further reading
