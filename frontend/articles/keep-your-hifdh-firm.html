Murajaah: preserving the Qur'an after memorisation

Why memorised text fades, what the Sunnah says about it, and how to build a revision system that keeps every juzʾ firm.

Himaayah Schools · 24 October 2026 · 8 min read

مُرَاجَعَةRevision

Memorisation (ḥifẓ) and revision (murājaʿah) are two halves of one discipline. Classical teachers treated the second as the measure of the first: a portion was not considered memorised until it could be recited correctly after time had passed. This article explains why memorised text decays, what the Prophetic guidance says about it, and how a revision system can be structured so that the whole Qur'an stays firm.

Why memorised text fades

Forgetting is not a sign of weak commitment; it is how memory behaves. In 1885 the psychologist Hermann Ebbinghaus measured how quickly people lost material they had learned and found that most of the loss happens soon after learning, after which it slows. Each successful review flattens that curve, so the interval before the next review can grow. Later research on distributed practice, synthesised by Cepeda and colleagues in 2006, confirmed that reviews spread over time produce far better long-term retention than the same amount of review done in one block.

For a ḥāfiẓ this has a direct consequence. The portions memorised most recently are the most fragile and need daily attention. Older portions are more stable but still decay if they are left untouched for weeks, and the decay usually appears first where verses resemble one another (al-mutashābihāt).

The three layers of a revision system

Qur'an teachers in many traditions divide a student's daily work into layers. The names vary, but the structure is consistent:

  • New memorisation: the current lesson, recited to the teacher until it is correct.
  • Recent revision: roughly the last juzʾ of memorised material, read every day because it has not yet settled.
  • Long-cycle revision: a fixed daily portion of older material, rotating so that everything memorised is covered within a set period.

For a student who has completed the Qur'an, the first layer disappears, and the danger is that the daily rhythm disappears with it. Murājaʿah replaces it: the long cycle becomes the core of the day, and recent revision becomes a focus on whichever portions the teacher has found weak.

Choosing the length of the cycle

The cycle should be set by consistency rather than ambition. One juzʾ a day completes the Qur'an in thirty days, two in fifteen, and five in about a week. A plan kept daily outperforms a heavier plan kept intermittently, which matches the Prophetic teaching:

Many students find that a modest daily portion, with one longer session each week, is sustainable alongside school or work.

A sample weekly plan

The following plan suits a student who has completed the Qur'an and has around forty-five minutes a day. It covers the whole Qur'an every fifteen days and leaves room for weak places.

  • Saturday to Thursday: two ajzāʾ of long-cycle revision each day, read aloud, one of them recited to a teacher or a revision partner.
  • Every day: ten minutes on the pages marked as weak in the previous cycle.
  • Friday: a lighter day for listening to a reciter and reviewing the error record.
  • Nightly: recite part of the day's revision in voluntary prayer.

The numbers matter less than the principle: a fixed daily load, a defined cycle, attention to weak places and regular recitation to someone else.

Recite to a teacher, not only to yourself

Self-revision catches gaps in memory, but it rarely catches errors in pronunciation, because a reciter tends to hear what they intend to say. Reciting to a qualified teacher (talaqqī) addresses both. The teacher hears hesitations, confusions between similar verses and slips in tajwīd, and can direct the student's revision toward those places. This is why the transmission of the Qur'an has always been oral as well as written.

Using prayer and listening

Reciting revised portions in voluntary prayers, particularly at night, tests whether the memory holds without the support of the muṣḥaf. Listening to a reliable reciter, ideally the same one consistently, reinforces correct pronunciation and the shape of each passage. Listening supports revision; it does not replace reciting aloud.

Marking and tracking weak places

An effective revision system is cumulative. Keep a simple record of where errors occur: the page and line, the type of error, and whether it recurs. Over a few cycles a pattern appears, usually a small number of pages that account for most mistakes, and these can be given extra attention.

Handling similar verses (al-mutashābihāt)

Most persistent errors in a ḥāfiẓ's recitation occur in passages that resemble passages elsewhere: a phrase that continues one way in one sūrah and another way in a second. Scholars wrote dedicated works on these verses because they are where memory most often slips. Three habits help.

  • When an error occurs between two similar passages, open both and compare them side by side until the difference is clear.
  • Attach the difference to something memorable: the sūrah's theme, the word that comes before, or the position on the page.
  • Revise the two passages close together for several days, then return to normal spacing.

When revision feels heavy

Every ḥāfiẓ meets periods when revision feels like a burden, particularly after the initial achievement has passed. Two things help most: reducing the daily portion rather than abandoning it, and reciting to a teacher or companion, which turns a private task into a shared commitment. Renewing the intention, that the Qur'an is preserved for the sake of Allah and recited in prayer, gives the work its meaning.

Revision for different kinds of students

Not every student revises under the same conditions. A school-going child may have only twenty or thirty minutes a day, and the plan should be built around that rather than around an ideal timetable. A working adult may have a long commute or a quiet hour at night. A hafidh who teaches others may revise naturally through teaching, but still needs time to recite alone. The principle is the same for all: decide the amount, fix the time, and protect it.

Students who travel often can use shorter portions, recited from memory while walking or waiting, and keep the longer sessions for the days when they are at home. What matters is that no day passes completely empty. A short portion recited every day is worth more than a long portion recited once a week.

Parents and revision

For a child who has memorised a large portion, parents can help without being hafidh themselves. They can protect the revision time, listen with the mushaf open and mark hesitations for the teacher, and ask the child to recite a short portion at a regular time each day. A child who sees that revision matters at home is more likely to treat it as part of life and not a chore for the teacher's sake.

What a teacher listens for

When a student recites for a teacher, the teacher listens for more than whether the words are right. Hesitation shows where memory is weak. Speed shows whether the portion is firm or only half-held. A change of tone before a verse may show that the student is bracing for a similar verse elsewhere. A good teacher notes these small signs and decides what to revise next, which is why a regular weekly check is so valuable.

A sample week of revision

A plan is easier to keep when it is written down. The week below is an example for a student who has memorised several juz and is still adding new portions. The amounts are only illustrative; a teacher should set them according to the student's level and time.

  • Every day: the new portion and the last few days of recent revision, recited aloud before the lesson, then once more after it.
  • Every day: one fixed rotating portion, for example a juz, so that older material is never left untouched for more than a few weeks.
  • Two days a week: recite a longer portion in the voluntary night prayer or in a quiet place, standing, without looking at the mushaf.
  • One day a week: recite to the teacher or to a trusted listener, and ask them to mark every hesitation, not only the clear mistakes.
  • One short session a week: revise the verses you confuse most often, side by side, until the difference is clear.

Mistakes that quietly weaken hifdh

Most students who lose portions did not stop revising altogether. They fell into habits that felt like revision but did not work. Three are especially common.

  • Reading from the mushaf and calling it revision. Looking at the page lets the eye do the remembering. Revision that tests the memory means reciting first and checking afterwards.
  • Always revising the strong portions. It is more pleasant to recite what flows easily, so weak places are quietly avoided. Mark them and give them more turns.
  • Adding new memorisation while the old is slipping. Speed is not the aim. A student who adds a page a day but loses two a week is going backwards. A teacher may slow the new portion until revision is steady.

When a portion feels like it is slipping

Almost every student reaches a point where a portion that was once firm suddenly feels unfamiliar. This is normal and is not a sign that the effort was wasted. Memory that has been learned once is usually recovered much faster than it was first built.

The way back is the same as the way forward: go back to that portion every day, recite it slowly, and use short passes rather than one long, frustrating one. If a whole juz has grown weak, it can be treated as new work for a few weeks, with a teacher checking it, before it returns to the normal cycle. Patience matters here more than anything else, because frustration usually leads to avoiding the portion, and avoidance is what weakens it further.

Indicators of firm memorisation

  • Beginning recitation from any verse on a page without looking.
  • Continuing correctly after an interruption.
  • Distinguishing similar passages in different sūrahs without hesitation.
  • Reciting long portions in prayer calmly.

Ḥifẓ is a trust (amānah) as much as an achievement. A structured murājaʿah plan, kept consistently and checked by a teacher, is how that trust is maintained.

Key points

  • Revise every day, with a fixed amount for recent and older portions.
  • Recite from memory first, check afterwards, and mark weak places.
  • Recite to a teacher regularly, and slow new memorisation if the old is slipping.
