Using past questions well

Why practising with past questions works, the common mistakes that waste it, and a method for turning each paper into progress.

Himaayah Schools · 24 October 2026 · 7 min read

تَدْرِيبPractice

Past questions are among the most popular tools for UTME, WAEC and NECO preparation, and for good reason. Practising retrieval under exam-like conditions is one of the best-supported learning strategies in cognitive psychology. Yet many students use past questions in a way that gives little benefit: reading questions with the answers visible, or memorising answers to specific questions. This article explains why testing works and how to use past papers properly.

Why testing yourself works

In a well-known experiment, Roediger and Karpicke had students either reread a passage several times or read it once and then take recall tests. After five minutes, rereading looked slightly better. After a week, the students who had tested themselves remembered substantially more. The effort of retrieving information strengthens the memory itself, and it also shows the student honestly what they do not yet know. Rereading feels productive but often produces an illusion of mastery.

Common mistakes

  • Looking at the answer before attempting the question.
  • Memorising the answers to specific questions instead of the principle behind them.
  • Doing many papers without reviewing the mistakes.
  • Practising only favourite subjects or topics.
  • Never practising under timed conditions.

A method for each paper

First, attempt the paper or section under real conditions: timed, without notes, on the same kind of screen or answer sheet you will use. Second, mark it honestly. Third, and most important, sort every wrong or guessed answer into a reason: a gap in knowledge, a misread question, a careless slip or running out of time. Each reason has a different remedy. Gaps send you back to the syllabus topic; misreading calls for slower reading of the stem; time problems call for practice in pacing.

Keep an error log

An error log is a notebook or spreadsheet where each mistake is recorded with the topic, the reason and the correct reasoning. Reviewing it weekly turns scattered mistakes into a clear list of priorities. Over time the log should shrink in some areas and reveal stubborn weaknesses in others, which is exactly the information a tutor needs to help.

Spacing and mixing practice

Two further findings from learning research improve past-question practice. Spacing means returning to a topic over several weeks instead of finishing it in one long session. Interleaving means mixing different types of question in one practice set, so the student must first recognise which method a question requires. Both feel harder than blocked practice, but both produce better long-term results, as reviewed by Dunlosky and colleagues.

A practical weekly pattern is to study new topics early in the week, then on one or two days take a mixed set of past questions covering both new and older topics, followed by a full timed paper every few weeks.

When to start using past questions

Many students leave past questions until the last weeks, but they are useful much earlier. Early on, a few questions on a topic show what examiners expect and where you are weak. Later, full papers under timed conditions train speed and stamina. A good pattern is topic questions during the study phase and full papers in the final phase.

Learning from a wrong answer

A wrong answer is information. For each one, ask three questions: did I not know the content, did I misread the question, or did I run out of time? The remedy is different in each case. A content gap needs study, a misreading needs slower reading and underlining key words, and a time problem needs practice under the clock. Writing the reason in the error log turns mistakes into a plan.

Do not memorise the answers

Questions are not repeated word for word. A student who memorises answers will fail when the wording changes, while a student who understands why an answer is correct can handle a new version of the same idea. After each practice question, make sure you could explain the answer to someone else.

Questions on the same topic: spotting patterns

After working through several years of papers, you will see patterns: topics that appear almost every year, favourite types of calculation, and typical traps in wording. Record these in a short list for each subject. This does not replace studying the whole syllabus, but it tells you where to give extra attention and how examiners think.

Using past questions in groups

A small group can use past questions well: each member attempts the paper alone under time, then the group compares answers, and anyone who got a question right explains how. Explaining is an excellent test of understanding. Keep the group focused and short, and finish with each person writing down what they learned.

Use the syllabus as the map

Past questions show the style and difficulty of an examination, but the official syllabus defines what can be asked. A student who prepares only from past papers may miss topics that have not appeared recently. The best approach combines both: study each syllabus topic, then test it with past questions, then log and repair the mistakes. Steady cycles of this kind over months are what produce strong results.

Simulating the real exam

A mock is most useful when it feels like the real thing. Sit at a table, switch off the phone, set a timer for the real length of the paper, and do not look up answers. Afterwards, mark honestly and record the score, the time spent on each section, and the questions that cost marks. Repeating this every week or two shows progress and builds calm.

Time management inside a paper

Divide the time by the number of questions before you start, and note where you should be at the halfway point. If a question takes too long, mark it and move on. It is better to attempt every question than to perfect half of them. In the last minutes, return to the marked questions and make informed guesses where there is no penalty for a wrong answer, checking the instructions first.

Learning from marking schemes and examiners' reports

Where marking schemes or chief examiners' reports are available, read them. They show what earns marks, what common mistakes cost marks and how answers should be presented. For written papers, comparing your answer with a model answer teaches you the structure examiners reward, which is often as important as the content.

Staying motivated through repetition

Practising past questions can become dull. Set small targets, such as beating last week's score or finishing a paper with ten minutes to spare, and mark them on a chart. Seeing progress turns repetition into a game against your own previous result.

Frequently asked questions

How many past papers should I do?

As many as you can review carefully; quality of review matters more than quantity.

Where can I find them?

Ask your teacher or school for trusted collections, and check that they match the current syllabus.

What if the questions seem too hard?

Start with topic questions and build up; difficulty usually falls as understanding grows.

Sorting questions by topic

After you have worked through several years of papers, sort the questions by topic instead of by year. You will see which areas appear again and again and which are rare. This tells you where your revision time brings the most return. It is not a reason to skip a topic entirely, because examiners vary what they ask, but it helps you decide what to practise more.

Turning a wrong answer into a short lesson

For every question you miss, write a three-line note: what the question asked, why your answer was wrong, and what the correct approach is. Then, a few days later, try a similar question without looking. If you can do it, the lesson has taken hold; if not, return to the topic. This small routine turns a stack of marked papers into a personal textbook built on your own mistakes.

Using a study group wisely

A small group can share the work of finding explanations. Each member attempts the paper alone first, then the group meets to compare answers and explain methods to each other. Teaching a method to a friend shows whether you truly understand it. Keep the group small, agree on a time limit, and avoid letting the session become a chat.

Knowing when you are ready

A useful sign of readiness is consistent scores on timed papers you have not seen before, together with fewer repeated mistakes in your error log. If your scores swing widely, you probably still have gaps in the basics. Continue practising calmly, and ask a teacher to look at the topics where you keep losing marks.

Using past questions in the final fortnight

In the last two weeks, shift from broad practice to targeted practice. Redo the questions in your error log, attempt one or two full papers under exam conditions, and review the formulas, definitions or rules you have found hardest to remember. Stop adding new sources; consolidate what you have. This focus lets you enter the exam with material you know well rather than a thin layer over many topics.

Key points

  • Use past questions early for topics and later for full timed papers.
  • Review every mistake and note its cause.
  • Understand the answers; do not memorise them.
