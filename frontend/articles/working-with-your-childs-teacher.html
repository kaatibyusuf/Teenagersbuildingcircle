Working with your child's teacher

What research says about family involvement, the kinds that make a difference, and how to build a useful partnership with teachers.

Himaayah Schools · 24 October 2026 · 2 min read

Children do better when the adults around them work together. Decades of research on family involvement in education point in the same direction, though the details matter: some forms of involvement help much more than others. This article summarises what the evidence says and offers practical ways for parents to work with teachers, including in online programmes.

What the evidence shows

In a widely used review of the research, Henderson and Mapp concluded that students whose families are involved in their learning tend to earn better grades, attend more regularly and show better behaviour, across backgrounds. The strongest effects came from involvement linked to learning itself: high expectations, conversations about schoolwork and support at home, rather than general attendance at events.

Involvement that makes a difference

  • Ask your child each day what they learned, and listen to them recite or explain it.
  • Know the weekly plan: what is being memorised, which topics are being taught, when the next test is.
  • Make sure homework and revision happen at a regular time and place.
  • Keep expectations high and express confidence that your child can meet them.

Communicating with teachers

Teachers value parents who share useful information early: a change at home, a health issue, a period of stress, or a concern about a particular subject. Messages that are specific and calm get better responses than general complaints. When a problem arises, ask what the teacher is seeing, share what you see at home and agree one or two actions each of you will take.

Using the student dashboard

Online programmes make involvement easier because records are visible. At Himaayah, each student's dashboard shows attendance, assignments, scores and payment status, and reminders arrive by email. A weekly five-minute look at the dashboard with your child, praising what is going well and noting what needs attention, is one of the simplest forms of involvement there is.

When there is a problem

If a child is struggling, falling behind or unhappy, contact the teacher early rather than waiting for results. Describe what you have noticed with specific examples, ask what the teacher has observed and agree a short plan with a date to review it. Following up after two or three weeks shows the child that the adults are working together and that the plan matters.

Equally, share good news. Teachers rarely hear when a lesson made a difference at home. A short message of thanks strengthens the relationship and makes it easier to solve problems when they arise.

Respecting roles

A good partnership respects each side's role. Parents know their child best; teachers know the subject and how children of that age typically learn it. Supporting the teacher in front of the child, and raising disagreements privately, protects the child's respect for both. In the Islamic tradition the teacher holds a place of honour, and children learn that honour largely from how their parents speak about teachers.

Sources and further reading

  2. Jeynes, W. H. (2007). The relationship between parental involvement and urban secondary school student academic achievement: A meta-analysis. Urban Education, 42(1), 82–110.
